Helping animators navigate the future of AI and animation.
Issue 04 · Jul 26, 2026 · 6 min read
This Week

AI's in the room now. Let's talk about what you build.

A few things landed this week that make the AI conversation feel less like a forecast and more like the present. AI was the thing nobody at Europe's biggest animation festival could stop talking about. The numbers on entry-level work got harder to ignore. And a good image generator went free inside Instagram and WhatsApp. None of it is a reason to panic, and none of it is a reason to cheer. This issue is the honest read, plus the two things worth building right now. Your own work, and your own eye.

INDUSTRY SIGNALS
 

The bottom rung is getting pulled up out of reach

If you're early in this career, or you teach people who are, this is the trend to actually watch. The entry point into animation is narrowing, and the data is now hard to wave off.

Start with the market. US animated series commissions peaked at 225 in 2021. In the first half of 2025 they came in at 71. That's a 68 percent drop in four years, tracked by Luminate and cited in the Animation Guild's Future Unscripted survey of 300 industry leaders, where the finding wasn't a forecast. It was a description of what has already happened. Zoom out past animation and a Stanford team found that for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed jobs, employment is now shrinking about 3.8 percent a year. Their phrase for it. It's eliminating the on-ramp.

The tools are the smaller story here. The bigger one is which jobs are thinning. Background, crowds, cleanup, previs, the assistant work, the exact reps where an animator's eye gets built. The Atlantic put animators, VFX artists, and storyboard artists among the roles most exposed. It also noted USC's Expanded Animation program shut down last month. When the training rung goes, the worry isn't only fewer jobs. It's fewer animators being made.

None of this is only AI. Studios were cutting and consolidating before the tools showed up, and some are using AI as cover for calls they'd already made. But the direction is clear enough to plan around.

So plan around it. If the studio ladder is missing its bottom rungs, you build your own. Go get the reps, put yourself next to people better than you every chance you get, and treat your eye as the asset it is. It's the one thing none of this touches, and in an AI world it's worth more, not less.

DAVE'S CORNER
 
D
David Torres
27 years in animation

Perspective

We all know how we grow as animators. Through mentorship, and by starting at the bottom on purpose. Crowds, background, a fix here and there, small pieces of a shot before anyone hands you a whole one. From the outside it's easy to dismiss as entry-level work. It's the opposite. That's the exact stretch where your eye gets built. You do the reps, you sit near people who've been at it far longer, and slowly you start to see. You feel when the timing is off, the weight being wrong, a pose saying the wrong thing. Nobody hands you that. You earn it one small piece at a time.

So what happens if a studio decides the cheapest way to get junior work done is to hand it to a machine? That isn't a question about the tool. It's a question about the ladder. Cut the bottom to save money and you haven't just cut some jobs. You've cut the place where animators are made. And it doesn't fall over today. It falls slowly. The veterans holding the ladder up retire, and if nobody came up behind them, the experience walks out the door with them. You feel it in ten years, not one.

I feel this one close to home. I have a kid who wants to make films and animate, and I want that rung to still be there for him. To do the small pieces, to be bad at it for a while, to sit next to someone better and take everything he can with his eyes.

I don't want to leave it there as just a worry, though. The eye you build doing the small work is the same eye that can look at anything a machine makes and know what's wrong with it. The person who did the reps sees it. The person who only ever prompted can't. So the entry point matters more in an AI world, not less. That's what I want to protect. Not by pretending the tools aren't here. By making sure the next person still gets to build the eye first.

◆  Field Kit · what I'd try this week

Never take a review note by hand again.

This one used to get me. I'd sit in dailies or a director review, the notes coming fast, and an hour later I'm staring at half a scribble trying to remember what they actually wanted. So I stopped writing them by hand. A notetaker catches the whole thing and my AI turns it into a clean record I can work from. Set it up once and every review after runs the same pass.

 

01  Let a notetaker catch the room

Connect a tool like Granola, Otter, or Fireflies to your calendar once and it joins the call, records both sides, and hands you a full transcript when it ends. You stay present instead of scribbling.

02  Hand the transcript to your AI

Copy the whole thing, paste it into Claude or ChatGPT, and let it hold the context while you ask for each piece below. You paste once and the rest runs off it.

03  Pull what got decided

Copy-paste prompt
Read this transcript. List every decision that was actually made, in plain language, with the one-line reason if it was stated. Put anything discussed but not decided under a separate heading, "Still open." Don't infer decisions that weren't clearly made. Transcript: [paste]

04  Pull the action items, with owners

Copy-paste prompt
From the same transcript, list every action item as Owner - Task - Due date. Only fill in an owner or date if it was actually said, write "?" where it wasn't, and don't guess. Flag at the top anything with no clear owner.

05  Catch what's still open

Ask for the questions nobody answered and anything parked for later, one line each, so the loose threads don't vanish before the next round.

06  File it, searchable

Have it combine the three into one dated note, tagged by shot, sequence, or project, in whatever you already search.

 

One more thing. Run a throwaway first. Type a fake note with one decision, one task, and one open question, push it through the steps, and check all three land in the right place before you trust it on a real review. If you caught the meeting-notes tip in my feed this week, this is the full build.
